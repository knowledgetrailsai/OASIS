# Chapter 18: Evaluation and Reliability Engineering

# Evaluation and Reliability Engineering

> **CHAPTER PURPOSE** Create the evidence system that measures response quality, agent behavior, workflow completion, resilience and business-outcome contribution.

## Background and context

Chapters 16 and 17 design who has authority over a piece of work and how the system reaches into enterprise applications to act on it. Neither chapter, by itself, answers the question that determines whether any of that design was actually sound: how do you know, with evidence rather than confidence, that the system does what it is supposed to do — before it ships, and continuously once it is live? That is this chapter's job, and it is deliberately paired around two questions that pull in the same direction from opposite ends: evaluation asks "how do we know this is good enough before it ships," and reliability engineering asks "what happens when it isn't, in production, right now." A system can have thorough evaluation coverage and still fail in the field if nothing contains the failures evaluation didn't anticipate; it can have extensive guardrails and still be untrustworthy if nothing measures whether it is actually accomplishing the task it was built for.

This chapter's direct implementation-level companion is [Engineering: Evaluation and Reliability Engineering](../engineering/evaluation-and-reliability-engineering.md), which turns the hierarchy and taxonomy below into a fifteen-dimension evaluation reference, an evaluation-progression ladder, a guardrails and recovery pattern library, and — worth flagging on its own — a grader-design and calibration checklist for the increasingly common practice of using one model to grade another's output ("LLM-as-judge"), which is only trustworthy once the judge has itself been checked against a sample of human-labeled cases and re-checked as it drifts. The [Evaluation Strategy and Dataset](../tools/02-workflow-and-intelligence-templates.md#9-evaluation-strategy-and-dataset) and [Failure Taxonomy](../tools/02-workflow-and-intelligence-templates.md#10-failure-taxonomy) templates are the fillable instruments behind the two tables in this chapter, and the [Observability and Telemetry Specification's](../monitoring/observability-and-telemetry-specification.md) intelligence plane is where the same quality dimensions this chapter defines offline get instrumented as production metrics with alert thresholds — evaluation does not stop at go-live, it becomes a sampled, continuous measurement of the same things tested before release.

The chapter that follows, Chapter 19, picks up where this one's reliability discipline leaves off: robustness testing against adversarial input here is the evaluation-side twin of the defense-in-depth security controls described there, and a failure discovered through evaluation is frequently the first evidence of a security gap rather than a purely quality one.

## Evaluation hierarchy

The most common evaluation mistake is picking one altitude — usually "is the model's response good" — and stopping there, because it is the easiest thing to test and the one closest to how a human naturally judges an AI system's output. It is also the least informative altitude on its own: a fluent, correct-sounding response proves nothing about whether the right tool was called with the right arguments, whether the multi-step task actually completed, or whether the business outcome moved at all. OASIS evaluates at six distinct levels, and a mature evaluation program maintains coverage at all of them rather than concentrating effort at the extremes.

| **Level**      | **Question**                        | **Examples**                                                  |
|----------------|-------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------|
| Component      | Does one layer work?                | Retrieval relevance, schema validity, classifier accuracy.    |
| Model response | Is the output correct and grounded? | Factuality, citation, completeness, abstention.               |
| Agent step     | Was the next action appropriate?    | Tool choice, arguments, policy and state update.               |
| Workflow       | Was the task completed correctly?   | End-to-end completion, exception handling, human effort.       |
| Service        | Is production performance reliable? | Latency, availability, incident, cost and drift.               |
| Outcome        | Did business performance improve?   | Conversion, MTTR, first-time-right, cycle time, loss avoided.  |

Each level answers a question the level above it cannot. A system that passes every component and model-response test can still fail at the agent-step level if it reliably picks the wrong tool for a case it otherwise understands correctly; a system that completes every workflow correctly in staging can still fail at the outcome level in production if the individually correct steps do not, in aggregate, move the metric the system was built to move. The [Engineering companion's evaluation-progression ladder](../engineering/evaluation-and-reliability-engineering.md#2-evaluation-progression-ladder) collapses this into four practical checkpoints — response, agent step, completed workflow, business outcome — mapped to where in the delivery lifecycle each is normally tested (dev, staging, pre-release, production) and who owns it; use it to decide, for any given quality question, which altitude is the right one to test at rather than defaulting to whichever is most convenient.

## Evaluation dataset strategy

An evaluation dataset that only contains the cases the system already handles well tells you nothing you did not already believe. A sound dataset deliberately combines curated gold cases (the clean, canonical examples of correct behavior), production samples (what actually arrives, which is rarely as clean as the gold set), historical failures (so a bug, once found, cannot silently reappear), boundary cases, prompts with genuinely insufficient evidence (to test whether the system abstains rather than fabricates), adversarial inputs, and counterfactual variants of the same case. Development, regression and held-out assurance sets need to stay separate and firewalled from each other — a case used to tune the system during development has no evidential value if it later shows up in the set used to certify the system is ready to ship, because at that point you are measuring memorization of the test, not generalized capability.

Two disciplines are easy to skip under delivery pressure and expensive to skip in practice. Leakage protection — making sure no case, or a near-duplicate of one, crosses from development into the held-out set — has to be actively enforced rather than assumed; and where a case has parent-case variants (the same underlying scenario tested with small perturbations), those variants belong in the same split, otherwise a system can appear to generalize well simply because it memorized one variant in training and was tested on a sibling. Every case should be versioned alongside its source evidence and its expected behavior, so that when the underlying policy or evidence changes, it is obvious which cases are now stale. The [Evaluation Strategy and Dataset template](../tools/02-workflow-and-intelligence-templates.md#9-evaluation-strategy-and-dataset) turns this into a fillable specification — fifteen quality dimensions, dataset composition and splits, per-case grader and pass threshold, a regression policy (every production failure becomes a case), and a production-sampling cadence — and is the artifact a build team should actually populate rather than reconstructing this section from prose each time.

## Failure taxonomy

Naming failures by family, before root-causing any individual incident, prevents the single most common diagnostic error in AI operations: assuming every strange output is "a model problem" when the responsible layer is frequently somewhere else entirely — a stale evidence source, a workflow that skipped a step, or an escalation trigger that never fired.

| **Failure family**   | **Illustrative failures**                                                              |
|-----------------------|------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Intent and scope     | Wrong task, wrong field, wrong lifecycle or unsupported expansion.                     |
| Evidence and context | Missing source, stale source, irrelevant context, poisoned context, unsupported claim. |
| Model behavior       | Reasoning error, hallucination, poor uncertainty, instruction failure.                 |
| Tool and action      | Wrong tool, invalid arguments, duplicate action, excessive authority.                  |
| Workflow and state   | Looping, skipped step, stale state, failed hand-off, incomplete recovery.              |
| Human interaction    | Unclear approval, automation bias, poor escalation, unusable correction.               |
| Control and policy   | Unauthorized access, policy bypass, incorrect retention, audit gap.                    |
| Service and outcome  | Latency, outage, cost spike, adverse downstream effect, no outcome lift.               |

This same taxonomy is what production operations classifies incidents against, at intake, before anyone starts root-causing — see the [Observability and Telemetry Specification's responsible-layer incident classification](../monitoring/observability-and-telemetry-specification.md#4-incident-classification-responsible-layer-taxonomy), which extends the families above into ten specific responsible layers (model, context, retrieval, tool selection, tool execution, state, workflow, policy, human approval, enterprise dependency) each routed to a named first responder. Using the same taxonomy in evaluation design and in production incident triage is deliberate: a failure family discovered in evaluation should map cleanly onto the same category an operations team would use to classify it live, and every incident that reaches production and gets root-caused should, in turn, become a new regression case in the dataset described above — via the [Failure Taxonomy template](../tools/02-workflow-and-intelligence-templates.md#10-failure-taxonomy), which adds severity, detectability, business impact, owner, containment action and confirmation that a regression case was actually added. A failure that is fixed but never added as a regression case is a failure the system is free to repeat.

## Reliability engineering

Evaluation tells you whether the system is good enough to ship. Reliability engineering is what happens after that judgment is made — the discipline of making sure the system degrades safely rather than catastrophically when something outside the evaluation set inevitably occurs. It starts with keeping two different kinds of objective genuinely separate: a service-level objective (is the system up, fast, within its error budget) and an intelligence-quality objective (is the system's output still correct and grounded) are not the same measurement, and a system can be meeting one while badly failing the other — a fast, always-available system returning confidently wrong answers is reliable by the first definition and dangerous by the second.

Reliability has to be tested deliberately, not assumed from the fact that nothing has broken yet: downstream outages, malformed tool output, model unavailability, context overflow and partial completion are all foreseeable failure modes, and each one needs a defined behavior rather than an undefined one discovered for the first time in production. The [Engineering companion's guardrails and recovery pattern library](../engineering/evaluation-and-reliability-engineering.md#3-guardrails-and-recovery-pattern-library) catalogues the standard responses — iteration and cost limits against runaway loops, safe retries restricted to idempotent operations, defined partial-completion states, compensating actions for anything non-idempotent, fallback models or workflows for degraded primaries, human escalation with an explicit trigger and target, and suspension or kill switches for anything requiring an immediate stop — each mapped to the specific failure mode it addresses and cross-referenced to the relevant control in the [Security checklist](../security/agentic-ai-threat-and-control-checklist.md#4-containment-and-emergency-control-checklist). High-consequence steps deserve a deterministic check, redundancy, or a mandatory human review rather than relying on the model's own judgment alone — the more expensive or irreversible the action, the less that decision should rest on a single non-deterministic judgment.

Error budgets give this discipline teeth: define an acceptable rate of service and quality degradation in advance, and restrict releases automatically once the budget is breached, rather than relying on a judgment call made under the pressure of an active incident. And every material change — a new model, a prompt edit, a reindexed knowledge base, a modified tool, a changed workflow, a revised policy — runs the full regression suite before release, exactly as the [Observability and Telemetry Specification's release manifest checklist](../monitoring/observability-and-telemetry-specification.md#3-release-manifest-checklist) requires: an immutable record of every version involved, automated regression, security, integration and policy tests passed, a rollout strategy with defined stop conditions, and a documented rollback-versus-forward-fix decision made before the release goes out, not improvised during it.

> **EVALUATION PRINCIPLE** A fluent response is not evidence of a successful workflow, and a successful workflow is not evidence of a valuable outcome.
