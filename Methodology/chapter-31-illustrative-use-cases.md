# Chapter 31: Illustrative Use Cases

# Illustrative Use Cases

> **CHAPTER PURPOSE** Show how the method changes across service, operations, knowledge, contracts, sentiment, maintenance, procurement and employee productivity use cases, and how the same underlying patterns recur across all of them.

## Background and context

The preceding thirty chapters describe OASIS as a method: phases, gates, artifacts, engineering disciplines, and the governance apparatus that holds them together. None of that shows what the method actually feels like on a live engagement, and a reader who has only seen the abstractions can be forgiven for wondering whether "outcome contract" and "autonomy matrix" are doing real work or just naming things that would happen anyway. This chapter closes that gap. It walks through eight representative use cases spanning customer service, back-office operations, knowledge work, legal review, sentiment and experience management, field maintenance, procurement, and individual productivity, and shows how the same six-phase lifecycle and the same disciplines from Part III land differently depending on what the system does and who it touches.

Chapter 30 established that OASIS scales its own depth to risk, autonomy, exposure, and maturity — the tailoring equation that keeps a small internal tool from carrying the assurance burden of a regulated, customer-facing agent. This chapter is the natural sequel: having established *how much* method to apply, it now shows *what that looks like* across genuinely different problem shapes. A voice-based appointment agent and a contract-review assistant both use OASIS, but they stress different parts of it — the appointment agent lives or dies on identity resolution, channel handling, and hand-off quality, while the contract reviewer lives or dies on clause retrieval fidelity, playbook discipline, and legal sign-off. Reading the two side by side is more instructive than reading either chapter's engineering discipline in isolation.

The use cases below are illustrative rather than exhaustive, and deliberately so. They are chosen to cover a spread of agent types and deployment shapes described in [Architecture: Agent Architecture](../architecture/perspective-02-agent-architecture.md) and [Architecture: Process Architecture](../architecture/perspective-03-process-architecture.md) — conversational agents, extraction-and-transaction agents, retrieval-and-advisory agents, and monitoring-and-recommendation agents — so that a reader building something not literally on this list can still find the nearest analog and borrow its pattern. Each row of the comparison table and each reusable pattern in the second section maps back to specific chapters earlier in the Methodology and to the fillable templates in [Chapter 32](chapter-32-templates-checklists-and-tools.md) and [Tools](../tools/01-outcome-and-portfolio-templates.md); use those links rather than treating this chapter as self-contained. It is meant to be read with the rest of the handbook open, not instead of it.

## Use-case patterns

Eight patterns recur often enough across industries and functions that they are worth walking through individually. Each one is described here by its outcome contract, its system emphasis, and the failure modes that matter most — the same three lenses an [Opportunity Assessment](chapter-32-templates-checklists-and-tools.md#1-opportunity-assessment) and [Value and Risk Case](chapter-32-templates-checklists-and-tools.md#5-value-and-risk-case) would apply to a real candidate.

The **customer-service appointment agent** exists to complete an eligible appointment and, in doing so, to reduce repeat calls and missed visits — a narrow, transactional outcome that is nonetheless one of the higher-stakes conversational patterns because it commits the business to a real-world action. Its system emphasis follows directly from that: intent recognition, policy application, live integration with CRM and field-service scheduling tools, handling across voice and chat channels, identity verification, and a confirmation-and-escalation path that a human can step into cleanly. The critical failures are equally concrete — an incorrect booking, a service promised to an ineligible customer, a privacy lapse during identity verification, or a hand-off to a human agent that loses context and forces the customer to repeat themselves. This pattern sits close to the [Human–AI Workflow Blueprint](chapter-32-templates-checklists-and-tools.md#7-human-ai-workflow-blueprint) and to the conversational-agent guidance in [Chapter 16 — Human–AI Workflow and Experience Engineering](chapter-16-human-ai-workflow-and-experience-engineering.md).

The **order-booking intelligence system** targets first-time-right booking accuracy and a shorter order-to-fulfillment cycle time. Its architecture is dominated by document and email ingestion, field extraction from unstructured or semi-structured input, mapping extracted values onto ERP transaction schemas, validation against business rules, and a review step before the transaction posts. Because this pattern writes directly into financial and operational systems of record, its failure modes are unforgiving: a wrong value silently accepted, a duplicate order created because deduplication logic missed a variant, an extraction mapped to a field the source document never actually supported, or an audit trail with a gap in it. This is squarely a [Data and Knowledge Readiness Assessment](chapter-32-templates-checklists-and-tools.md#8-data-and-knowledge-readiness-assessment) and [Evaluation Strategy and Dataset](chapter-32-templates-checklists-and-tools.md#9-evaluation-strategy-and-dataset) problem before it is a modeling problem — extraction quality is bounded by source-document quality and by how well the evaluation set represents the messy real distribution of incoming documents, not by the model alone.

The **contract analysis and review** pattern aims for faster review cycles that still land on an acceptable risk position — speed without a corresponding erosion of legal judgment. Its system emphasis is clause retrieval against a governed corpus, application of a negotiation playbook, structured issue-spotting output that a lawyer can scan quickly, and deterministic (not generative) redlining constrained to pre-approved language, with legal approval remaining the final authority. The failure modes read like a list of things that would alarm a general counsel: an invented clause that does not exist in the source contract, a jurisdictional error that applies the wrong governing-law assumptions, or an unauthorized change slipping into redlined text without review. This is one of the clearer illustrations in the handbook of why [Chapter 14's workflow-pattern decision rule](chapter-14-intelligence-and-agent-engineering.md) matters — retrieval-and-extraction plus deterministic redlining is a defensible design where an open-ended generative rewrite of contract language would not be.

The **sentiment and next-best-action** pattern is built for earlier issue detection and better closed-loop recovery — catching a deteriorating customer relationship before it becomes a churn event or a complaint. It draws on multi-channel ingestion (calls, chat, surveys, social), identity resolution, a sentiment and issue taxonomy, trend analysis over time, and a recommendation surfaced into a CRM action a human executes. Because this pattern classifies people, its failure modes carry a distinct ethical weight: biased classification, misclassification that misroutes a customer, a design that edges into surveillance rather than service, or acting on an unverified identity match. Anyone building this pattern should read it alongside [Chapter 19 — Security and Responsible AI Engineering](chapter-19-security-and-responsible-ai-engineering.md) and the fairness and bias-testing guidance referenced there, and should expect the [Autonomy Matrix](chapter-32-templates-checklists-and-tools.md#12-autonomy-matrix) to keep any customer-facing action at a supervised or assisted level rather than autonomous, at least until evidence justifies otherwise.

**Predictive maintenance and field service** aims to reduce unplanned downtime and improve first-time fix rates. It combines telemetry ingestion, a prediction or anomaly-detection layer, retrieval against equipment manuals and service history, parts and tooling availability data, and integration into the technician's actual workflow and work-order system — a pattern that fails quietly if it is accurate in the lab but never actually reaches the technician at the point of decision. Its critical failures are physical-world failures: an unsafe recommendation, sensor drift that degrades prediction quality without anyone noticing, or a false negative that misses a failure the system was built to catch. This pattern belongs in conversation with [Architecture: Deployment Architecture](../architecture/perspective-07-deployment-architecture.md) where edge and connectivity constraints shape what telemetry can even reach the system in time to matter.

The **enterprise knowledge assistant** targets faster, authorized answers with lower search effort — arguably the most common first intelligence system an enterprise builds, and also one of the easiest to get wrong in ways that are invisible until an incident exposes them. Its emphasis is access-filtered retrieval (a user should never see an answer built from a document they are not authorized to read), citation of sources, a willingness to abstain rather than guess, freshness of the underlying index, and a feedback loop that lets users flag wrong or stale answers. Its failure modes — data leakage across access boundaries, stale policy presented as current, and false confidence in an unsupported answer — are exactly what [Chapter 18 — Evaluation and Reliability Engineering](chapter-18-evaluation-and-reliability-engineering.md) means by groundedness and calibration, and why access control is treated as a retrieval-time concern rather than a downstream filter.

**Procurement and supplier intelligence** pursues better cycle time, compliance, and supplier-risk visibility. It draws together sourcing data, contract terms, policy constraints, external information about supplier risk, an approval workflow, and ERP integration for the resulting transactions. Its failure modes include unfair or opaque supplier scoring, reliance on unsupported external data (a risk signal pulled from an unverified source and treated as fact), and authority conflicts where the system's recommendation and an existing delegation-of-authority policy disagree. This is a natural candidate for the [Risk and Control Register](chapter-32-templates-checklists-and-tools.md#17-risk-and-control-register) given how directly it touches financial commitment and vendor relationships.

The **employee productivity agent** — drafting, summarizing, scheduling, and retrieving across a knowledge worker's own documents, email, and calendar — aims to reduce task time while holding quality steady. Its system emphasis is almost entirely about respecting boundaries: user context handled correctly, document and communication access scoped tightly, and a design that keeps the employee in control of what the agent can see and do on their behalf. Its failure modes follow from that emphasis inverted: oversharing information across a boundary the user did not intend to cross, an accidental action taken without sufficiently explicit confirmation, or a productivity claim that nobody actually measured. This is the pattern most organizations attempt first, precisely because it looks low-risk — and the failure modes above are exactly why "low-risk" is not the same as "no governance," a distinction Chapter 30's tailoring equation is built to make explicit.

## Cross-business reusable patterns

Read the eight use cases side by side and a shorter list of recurring building blocks emerges — components that show up in most intelligence systems regardless of industry or function, and that are consequently worth engineering once and reusing rather than reinventing per project. Identity resolution and authorization-aware context construction appears in the appointment agent, the sentiment pattern, and the knowledge assistant alike: whenever a system needs to know who it is dealing with and what that person or account is entitled to see, the same resolution and authorization logic applies. Document ingestion, structured extraction, and source citation form the backbone of the order-booking, contract-review, and knowledge-assistant patterns — different documents, different downstream use, but the same underlying extraction-and-attribution discipline.

Enterprise search and knowledge retrieval underpins not just the knowledge assistant but also the contract reviewer's clause lookup and the maintenance technician's manual lookup — retrieval quality, once built well, is an asset the rest of the portfolio can draw on rather than a bespoke component per system. Approval, exception, and escalation workflow recurs everywhere a human needs to remain the final authority on a consequential decision, from procurement sign-off to a customer-service transfer. Action confirmation, idempotency, and transaction reconciliation matter anywhere a system writes to a system of record — order booking and procurement most obviously, but any pattern that books, cancels, or modifies something real needs the same discipline against duplicate or lost transactions.

An evaluation harness, a failure taxonomy, and outcome telemetry are not use-case-specific at all; every pattern above needs them, and [Chapter 18](chapter-18-evaluation-and-reliability-engineering.md) and the [Failure Taxonomy](chapter-32-templates-checklists-and-tools.md#10-failure-taxonomy) template are written to be reused verbatim across all eight rather than redesigned per project. Voice transcription, call summarization, and hand-off logic serve the appointment agent and the sentiment pattern together. And agent trace, audit, incident, and cost measurement — the operational instrumentation described in [Chapter 21](chapter-21-deployment-operations-and-agentops.md) and the [Monitoring: Observability and Telemetry Specification](../monitoring/observability-and-telemetry-specification.md) — is the connective tissue that lets an enterprise running many of these systems at once actually see what is happening across the portfolio rather than one dashboard per project.

Recognizing these as reusable components rather than accidental similarities is itself a scaling decision, and it connects directly to [Chapter 28 — Scaling and Productization](chapter-28-scaling-and-productization.md) and the [Scale and Productization Assessment](chapter-32-templates-checklists-and-tools.md#18-scale-and-productization-assessment) template: a component built once for the appointment agent's identity resolution and reused for the sentiment pattern's identity resolution is a candidate for platform investment, not a coincidence to shrug off.

## Example outcome contract

The table below shows a worked outcome contract for the appointment-agent pattern, populated at the level of detail a real engagement would carry into a [Phase 1: Engage & Align](chapter-07-phase-1-engage-and-align.md) kickoff. It is deliberately concrete rather than templated, so that a reader comparing it against the blank [Outcome Contract](chapter-32-templates-checklists-and-tools.md#3-outcome-contract) template in [Tools: Outcome and Portfolio Templates](../tools/01-outcome-and-portfolio-templates.md) can see what a filled field actually looks like versus a placeholder.

| **Field**          | **Appointment-agent example**                                                                  |
|--------------------|------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Population         | Eligible installation and service calls in defined product and geography.                      |
| Primary outcome    | Percentage of calls ending in a valid confirmed appointment without agent transfer.            |
| Baseline / target  | Measured current baseline; target set after representative validation.                         |
| Leading indicators | Intent resolution, slot availability, confirmation and hand-off quality.                       |
| Guardrails         | Incorrect bookings, complaints, repeat calls, privacy incidents and service-policy violations. |
| Authority          | May propose and book within policy; exceptions transfer to human.                              |
| Measurement        | Call-centre, CRM and field-service records reconciled by booking ID.                           |
| Review             | Weekly during pilot; monthly after stable operation.                                           |

Notice how directly each row maps onto a chapter or template already covered: population and primary outcome are [Outcome Charter](chapter-32-templates-checklists-and-tools.md#2-outcome-charter) fields; guardrails and authority anticipate the [Autonomy Matrix](chapter-32-templates-checklists-and-tools.md#12-autonomy-matrix); measurement and review cadence anticipate the [Outcome Scorecard](chapter-32-templates-checklists-and-tools.md#15-outcome-scorecard) and the review rhythm set out in [Chapter 27 — Delivery Cadence and Management Practices](chapter-27-delivery-cadence-and-management-practices.md). The next chapter, [Chapter 32](chapter-32-templates-checklists-and-tools.md), is where every one of these fields becomes a fillable artifact rather than a worked example — read this contract as the "why it looks like this," and the next chapter as the "here is the blank form."
