# Chapter 26: OASIS Measurement Framework

# OASIS Measurement Framework

> **CHAPTER PURPOSE** Use a balanced scorecard that joins business outcomes with adoption, intelligence quality, risk, reliability, economics, reuse and FDE effectiveness.

## Background and context

Part V opens with a deliberate shift in altitude. The chapters that precede it — through Chapter 25's treatment of the Enterprise Intelligence Platform — are largely about building: how a pod stands up an intelligence system, how a platform accumulates shared capability underneath it. This chapter, and the four that follow it, are about running what has been built and knowing, with evidence rather than impression, whether it should keep running. That distinction matters because a portfolio of intelligence systems can look busy — releases shipping, dashboards populated, users logging in — while quietly failing to produce the outcomes it was funded to produce. The measurement framework exists to close that gap between activity and value.

It also exists because intelligence systems fail in ways that conventional software rarely does, and a measurement approach built purely around uptime and throughput will not catch them. A system can be fast, available and technically correct in every request-response cycle and still be wrong in a way that only shows up in aggregate: quietly biased toward one segment of users, gradually eroding trust so that people route around it, or costing more in human intervention than it saves in automation. The eight dimensions in the scorecard below are the enterprise's answer to that risk — a way of looking at a system from enough angles simultaneously that a problem in one dimension cannot hide behind good numbers in another.

This chapter sits upstream of two companions that carry its framework into daily practice, and it is worth reading them alongside it rather than instead of it. The [Monitoring: Observability and Telemetry Specification](../monitoring/observability-and-telemetry-specification.md) takes the operational and intelligence-quality dimensions introduced here and turns them into the six instrumented planes — service, intelligence, risk, human, economic and outcome — with concrete collection points and alert triggers a delivery team can actually implement; where this chapter tells you what to measure and why, that specification tells you where in the system to measure it and what should page someone. The [Outcome Metric Tree](../tools/01-outcome-and-portfolio-templates.md#4-outcome-metric-tree) template gives each initiative a place to define its own primary outcome, leading indicators, operational drivers and guardrails in the structure this chapter describes, and the [Outcome Scorecard](../tools/04-readiness-and-operations-templates.md#15-outcome-scorecard) is the recurring artifact that reports against it. Chapter 27, immediately following, takes the cadences introduced here — daily, monthly, quarterly, event-driven — and builds the full delivery rhythm and backlog structure around them.

## Balanced OASIS scorecard

No single number tells an organization whether an intelligence system is working. A model that answers correctly ninety percent of the time can still be a poor investment if the ten percent of failures land on the highest-stakes cases, or if the cost of catching and correcting them exceeds the value the other ninety percent creates. The balanced scorecard exists to prevent exactly this kind of false confidence by requiring evidence across eight dimensions before anyone declares success, and it deliberately spans further than a conventional software scorecard would, because an intelligence system's failure modes are not confined to availability and defect rate.

Business outcome sits at the center: the primary metric the initiative was funded to move, together with the leading indicators that give early warning before the primary metric shifts, the distribution of that outcome across segments and populations, the guardrails that catch unacceptable side effects, and — critically — an explicit statement of attribution confidence, since claiming credit for a movement the system did not actually cause is one of the most common ways a scorecard misleads its own sponsors. Adoption and operations describe whether people actually use the system as intended: eligible use against active use, completion rates, time genuinely saved once rework is subtracted out, and the volume of overrides and exceptions that signal either a trust problem or a real capability gap. Intelligence quality is the layer specific to AI systems — correctness, groundedness of claims against source evidence, retrieval precision, whether tools were selected and invoked correctly, end-to-end task completion, and two measures that are easy to omit and important not to: abstention (does the system know when not to answer) and calibration (when it expresses confidence, is that confidence justified).

Risk and guardrails track the things a scorecard exists to prevent, not merely to describe: incorrect actions actually taken, complaints, policy breaches, indicators of biased treatment across populations, incidents and the near misses that preceded them before something worse happened. Service and reliability cover the ground a conventional operations dashboard already covers well — availability, latency, error rates, recovery time, capacity headroom and the health of upstream dependencies — and remain necessary even though they are no longer sufficient on their own. Economics brings cost into the same conversation as value: cost per successful outcome, the cost of human intervention when the system needs correcting, a failure-adjusted view of value that does not pretend failures are free, realized savings, and the marginal cost of adding the next unit of scale. Platform reuse asks whether the investment compounds: time saved through shared components, adoption of shared services, measurable reduction in duplicate builds, and the number of consumers a shared capability actually supports — this is the dimension that connects most directly to Chapter 28's treatment of scaling and productization. FDOE effectiveness, finally, measures the pod itself: how quickly it reaches a working vertical slice, how quickly that reaches production, how fast it is learning, how ready its work is for transfer to a permanent owner, and how much the business still depends on the pod to keep operating what should by now be self-sustaining.

| **Dimension**           | **Representative measures**                                                                                 |
|-------------------------|-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Business outcome        | Primary outcome, leading indicators, distribution, guardrails and attribution confidence.                   |
| Adoption and operations | Eligible use, active use, completion, time saved, rework, override and exception load.                      |
| Intelligence quality    | Correctness, groundedness, retrieval, tool use, task completion, abstention and calibration.                |
| Risk and guardrails     | Incorrect actions, complaints, policy breaches, bias indicators, incidents and near misses.                 |
| Service and reliability | Availability, latency, errors, recovery, capacity and dependency health.                                    |
| Economics               | Cost per success, intervention cost, failure-adjusted value, savings and marginal scale cost.               |
| Platform reuse          | Time saved, shared-service adoption, duplicate reduction and supported consumers.                           |
| FDOE effectiveness      | Time to vertical slice, time to production, learning velocity, transfer readiness and dependency reduction. |

## Metric architecture

A scorecard with eight dimensions is only coherent if the metrics inside it relate to one another in a defined way, rather than sitting side by side as an unstructured list. OASIS organizes every metric tree around a primary outcome — the one number the initiative exists to move — supported by leading indicators that change earlier and faster than the outcome itself, and bounded by guardrails that exist purely to catch harm the primary metric would not otherwise reveal. Operational and intelligence-quality measures sit underneath all of this, not because they matter less, but because their job is explanatory: when the outcome moves, they are what let a team say why it moved, rather than simply that it did. A leading indicator that never actually leads — one that moves without the outcome subsequently following — is not doing its job and should be revisited or retired rather than kept out of habit.

Every metric in the tree needs seven things before it is trustworthy enough to act on: a named owner, a precise definition, a data source, a reporting cadence, a target, a segmentation so that an average cannot hide a disparity underneath it, and an action threshold that specifies what happens when the metric crosses it. A metric without an action threshold is a data point people look at; a metric with one is a control people can rely on to trigger a decision. The [Outcome Metric Tree](../tools/01-outcome-and-portfolio-templates.md#4-outcome-metric-tree) template captures exactly this structure — primary outcome, leading indicators, operational drivers and guardrails, each with definition, source, segmentation and threshold — and its guidance is explicit that a tree with no guardrail row is incomplete, since every outcome pursued through an intelligence system needs at least one metric built specifically to catch an unacceptable side effect.

One discipline is worth stating plainly because it is easy to violate with good intentions: automation that shifts cost, risk or workload elsewhere in the organization is not counted as value here. A process that looks faster because exceptions are now silently dumped on a downstream team, or because risk has been quietly transferred to a function without the visibility to manage it, has not created value — it has relocated a cost and hidden it from the very scorecard meant to surface it. Treating that relocation as a genuine efficiency gain is one of the more common and more damaging measurement errors an intelligence program can make, and the metric architecture above is built specifically to make it harder to get away with.

## Outcome reporting cadence

Different questions need to be answered at different speeds, and the measurement framework accepts that rather than forcing every metric onto a single reporting rhythm. A daily or weekly operational review looks at incidents, quality shifts and exceptions — the things that need a response measured in hours or days, not the next scheduled meeting. A monthly outcome review joins business, user, intelligence, risk, service and cost evidence into a single picture of whether the initiative is actually working, which is deliberately the same cross-dimensional view the balanced scorecard above describes. A quarterly portfolio review steps back further still, to investment, dependencies, reuse, scale and retirement decisions that only make sense evaluated across a longer horizon and, usually, across more than one initiative at a time. And an event-driven review triggers outside any fixed schedule whenever something material changes — a new model, a new provider, a change in the data feeding the system, a new jurisdiction, a change in purpose, or a change in the level of autonomy the system is permitted — because waiting for the next scheduled review to notice a change of that magnitude is itself a control failure.

This cadence structure is not incidental to Chapter 26; it is the backbone Chapter 27 builds its entire delivery rhythm around. The daily, monthly and quarterly reviews described here reappear there as concrete ceremonies with defined attendees, inputs and outputs, and the [Outcome Scorecard](../tools/04-readiness-and-operations-templates.md#15-outcome-scorecard) template is explicit that it should be reviewed at the cadence Chapter 27 sets. At enterprise scale, this same cadence also feeds the rollup views described in [Architecture Perspective 9 — Operations and Observability Architecture](../architecture/perspective-09-operations-and-observability-architecture.md#1-enterprise-operations-dashboard-rollup-structure), where per-system outcome scorecards and monitoring data aggregate into a portfolio-level view reviewed by governance forums rather than by a single delivery team.

## Evidence-based continuation

The scorecard is not an end in itself; its purpose is to make four kinds of decision defensible with evidence rather than opinion. The dashboard supports continuing an initiative because value and controls are demonstrably stable — the boring, desirable outcome that most mature systems should reach. It supports correcting course when the underlying outcome remains viable but performance has degraded, which is the everyday work of the delivery cadence described in the next chapter. It supports re-scoping when the evidence shows the causal mechanism was wrong from the start, or the boundary of the problem was drawn incorrectly — a conclusion that is uncomfortable to reach honestly but far less costly than reaching it late. And it supports stopping, when sustained evidence shows that value, safety or economics simply do not hold up, regardless of how much has already been invested. A measurement framework that cannot produce a credible "stop" decision is not really measuring anything — it is decoration around a decision that was already made elsewhere.
