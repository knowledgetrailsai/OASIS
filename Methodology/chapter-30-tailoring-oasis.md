# Chapter 30: Tailoring OASIS

# Tailoring OASIS

> **CHAPTER PURPOSE** Control methodology depth by organizational size, solution risk, autonomy, data sensitivity, exposure, scale and maturity while preserving essential controls.

## Background and context

Every chapter before this one describes the full OASIS methodology at its fullest depth — every artifact, every register, every review cadence. Applied literally and uniformly to every initiative regardless of size or risk, that full depth would sink a small pilot under paperwork it does not need and, just as dangerously, might tempt a high-risk initiative to treat the same reduced footprint as adequate simply because a smaller organization used it successfully elsewhere. This chapter exists to resolve that tension directly: it gives every other chapter in this handbook a principled way to be applied at less than full depth, without ever making that reduction arbitrary or convenient rather than justified.

Chapter 30 follows naturally from [Chapter 29's adoption roadmap](chapter-29-oasis-adoption-roadmap.md), which already tailors instinctively — a first ninety-day pilot borrows specialist capability and combines artifacts rather than standing up the full governance structure this handbook describes elsewhere. This chapter makes that instinct explicit and repeatable, so that tailoring is a deliberate, recorded decision rather than something a team backs into by omission. It is also the last methodology chapter before [Chapter 31's illustrative use cases](chapter-31-illustrative-use-cases.md), which is not a coincidence: the worked examples that follow only make sense once the reader understands that each one reflects a tailoring decision, not a fixed template applied without judgment.

This chapter's framework is used directly, and by name, elsewhere in this repository. The [OASIS Reference Architecture's tailoring section](../architecture/oasis-reference-architecture.md#5-tailoring-this-architecture) states explicitly that architectural tailoring — which components of the reference architecture's system diagram a simpler system may legitimately omit — is governed by this chapter's framework, not by the reference architecture document itself. That is precisely the relationship this chapter is written to support: the tailoring equation below decides how much depth an initiative needs, and every other artifact in the handbook, architectural or otherwise, defers to that decision rather than setting its own independent threshold.

## Tailoring equation

> **REQUIRED ASSURANCE** f(impact, autonomy, data sensitivity, external exposure, scale, regulatory obligation, reversibility and organizational maturity).

The equation above is deliberately not a formula anyone can plug numbers into and get a single output; it is a statement about which variables are allowed to influence required assurance and, just as importantly, which are not. Organizational size is conspicuously absent from that list, and that absence is the chapter's central claim: size affects how work is staffed and packaged — a small organization will combine roles and artifacts that a large one keeps separate — but it does not, on its own, determine how much assurance a given piece of work requires. A small manufacturer using AI to support a safety-critical engineering decision may need materially deeper assurance than a large enterprise deploying an internal meeting-summary assistant, precisely because impact and reversibility, not headcount, are doing the work in the equation. Treating organizational size as a proxy for required rigor is the single most common tailoring mistake this chapter is written to prevent.

The six implementation levels below translate that equation into a scale a team can actually place an initiative on, from early exploration through to critical autonomy. L0, Exploration, is about establishing technical possibility using synthetic or non-sensitive data, a defined experiment, an explicit prohibited-use boundary and basic evaluation — the lightest tier, appropriate only while nothing resembling a real decision is yet at stake. L1, PoC, tests the outcome and intelligence hypothesis itself, and needs an outcome hypothesis, representative cases, an initial risk screen and prototype-level controls — recognizably the work of [Chapter 8's Discover & Validate phase](chapter-08-phase-2-discover-and-validate.md). L2, Controlled pilot, moves to real users and real data and correspondingly needs a privacy and security review, defined access, genuine human oversight, monitoring and a formal pilot acceptance decision before it proceeds further.

L3, Production service, is where a system starts supporting a live business process, and the minimum bar rises accordingly: production architecture, automated tests, AgentOps discipline, a runbook, and named service and outcome ownership — everything Chapter 27's delivery cadence and Chapter 26's measurement framework assume is already in place. L4, Enterprise scale, deploys the capability across multiple business units on shared infrastructure, and needs platform services, planned capacity, federated governance, version support and active portfolio management, which is where this chapter's framework meets [Chapter 28's](chapter-28-scaling-and-productization.md) treatment of scaling and productization directly. L5, Critical autonomy, is reserved for systems executing consequential decisions or actions on their own authority, and demands enhanced validation, independent assurance separate from the delivery team, strictly bounded authority, continuous evidence, and emergency controls that can actually be exercised under pressure, not merely documented.

| **Implementation level** | **Purpose**                                   | **Minimum engineering and governance**                                                                     |
|--------------------------|-----------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| L0 — Exploration          | Understand technical possibility.             | Synthetic/non-sensitive data, experiment definition, prohibited-use boundary and basic evaluation.             |
| L1 — PoC                  | Test outcome and intelligence hypothesis.     | Outcome hypothesis, representative cases, risk screen and prototype controls.                                  |
| L2 — Controlled pilot     | Validate with selected users and real data.   | Privacy/security review, access, human oversight, monitoring and pilot acceptance.                             |
| L3 — Production service   | Support a live business process.              | Production architecture, automated tests, AgentOps, runbook, service and outcome ownership.                    |
| L4 — Enterprise scale     | Deploy across units and shared capabilities.  | Platform services, capacity, federated governance, version support and portfolio management.                   |
| L5 — Critical autonomy    | Execute consequential decisions or actions.   | Enhanced validation, independent assurance, strict authority, continuous evidence and emergency controls.      |

## Organization-size patterns

Organizational size shapes the *form* tailoring takes even though, as established above, it should not shape the *depth* of assurance an initiative requires. A small organization typically operates with one cross-functional team, combined artifacts, managed third-party services rather than in-house platform build-out, combined governance gates, and short decision records — a leaner package overall, but one that still cannot sacrifice a defined set of non-negotiables: an accountable outcome owner, an honest risk classification, evaluation against representative cases rather than convenient ones, explicit authority limits, a working fallback path, and monitoring that actually runs. A small organization that drops any of these to save effort has not tailored the methodology — it has simply removed a control and hoped not to need it.

A medium-sized organization generally supports a small portfolio office, shared platform defaults it does not have to reinvent per initiative, specialist reviews brought in on demand rather than staffed permanently, and monthly outcome governance rather than a heavier cadence. What it must not remove is production ownership, regression testing discipline, access controls, a working incident process, and genuine value accounting — the difference from the small-organization list reflecting that a medium-sized organization typically runs enough concurrent initiatives that informal versions of these controls stop being reliable. A large organization or group typically needs federated business ownership paired with a central platform and enterprise standards, multiple pods running in parallel, and formal service and portfolio governance to keep all of that coherent. At this scale, what must not be compromised is clear decision rights so accountability does not blur across the federation, proper tenancy so one business unit's data and one business unit's incident stay contained to that unit, real productization discipline so shared capability does not silently fork per consumer, and independent assurance wherever the risk classification actually warrants it.

| **Organization** | **Recommended form**                                                                                                  | **Do not remove**                                                                                          |
|--------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Small              | One cross-functional team; combined artifacts; managed services; combined gates; short decision records.            | Outcome owner, risk classification, representative evaluation, authority limits, fallback and monitoring.  |
| Medium             | Small portfolio office; shared platform defaults; specialist reviews on demand; monthly outcome governance.         | Production ownership, regression, access controls, incident process and value accounting.                  |
| Large / group      | Federated business ownership; central platform and standards; multiple pods; formal service and portfolio governance. | Clear decision rights, tenancy, productization discipline, independent assurance where warranted.          |

## Solution archetype tailoring

Where organizational size shapes the packaging of governance, the solution's archetype shapes which specific risks deserve the closest attention, and treating every archetype identically wastes effort on the wrong things while leaving the right things under-examined. A small productivity use needs relatively little formal structure but should not skip privacy, clear user guidance, adoption tracking, cost control and attention to data leakage, with only light workflow integration required beyond that. A knowledge assistant lives or dies on source authority, retrieval that respects access controls rather than surfacing whatever is easiest to find, accurate citation, a working abstention behavior, and freshness of the underlying knowledge base — get any of those wrong and the assistant becomes actively misleading rather than merely unhelpful.

A customer-facing conversational system carries a different weight entirely: disclosure that the user is talking to an AI, service quality that holds up under real conversational variety, a genuinely working escalation path to a human, language handling, identity verification where relevant, and disciplined complaint handling, because failures here are visible to the customers the business exists to serve. Operational automation shifts the emphasis toward tool contracts that behave predictably, transactional integrity, idempotency so a retried action does not duplicate its effect, recovery from partial failure, capacity planning, and clear process ownership — this is largely an engineering-reliability problem wearing an AI label. Decision support systems need evidence quality, calibrated confidence, active attention to bias, preserved human authority over the final call, genuine contestability of that decision, and decision records that hold up under later scrutiny.

Voice agents add a layer most text-based systems do not have to think about: consent and notice given verbally rather than in writing, transcription accuracy, graceful handling of interruption, latency the caller can tolerate in real time, a clean hand-off to a human, and a defined sense of what counts as a successful call outcome. Multi-agent systems introduce risks that simply do not exist in a single-agent design — inter-agent trust, shared state, isolation between agents so one compromised agent cannot silently compromise another, failure propagation across the system, orchestration correctness, and containment when something goes wrong in a way that a single agent's guardrails were never designed to catch. High-risk or regulated solutions, finally, need everything above plus explicit legal applicability analysis, a formal impact assessment, independent assurance separate from the delivery team, durable records, and strictly bounded authority — the combination this chapter's L5 tier and the tailoring equation's regulatory-obligation variable exist specifically to enforce.

| **Archetype**                  | **Emphasize**                                                                                  |
|----------------------------------|-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Small productivity use         | Privacy, user guidance, adoption, cost and data leakage; light workflow integration.            |
| Knowledge assistant            | Source authority, access-filtered retrieval, citation, abstention and freshness.                |
| Customer-facing conversation   | Disclosure, service quality, escalation, language, identity and complaint handling.              |
| Operational automation         | Tool contracts, transactions, idempotency, recovery, capacity and process ownership.             |
| Decision support                | Evidence, calibration, bias, human authority, contestability and decision records.               |
| Voice agent                     | Consent/notice, transcription, interruption, latency, hand-off and call outcome.                 |
| Multi-agent system              | Inter-agent trust, state, isolation, propagation, orchestration and containment.                 |
| High-risk or regulated          | Legal applicability, impact assessment, independent assurance, records and strict authority.     |

## Tailoring record

Tailoring is a decision, not a default, and OASIS treats it as one that has to be written down rather than inferred later from whatever artifacts happen to exist. Every initiative records the level it operates at, its risk profile, which workstreams from across this handbook are actually activated, which artifacts have been deliberately combined or omitted and why, its gate cadence, and — most importantly — the rationale behind all of it, so a later reviewer can tell the difference between a considered reduction in scope and simple neglect. This record is what makes the [OASIS Reference Architecture's own tailoring section](../architecture/oasis-reference-architecture.md#5-tailoring-this-architecture) workable in practice: when that document says a deterministic-function-only system may omit the Model, Memory and multi-agent components of the reference architecture but must still complete controls, output validation and runtime tracing, it is this chapter's tailoring record that captures the justification for exactly that pattern of inclusion and omission, tied to a specific initiative rather than asserted in the abstract.

Tailoring is not a one-time decision made at kickoff and then forgotten. It is revisited whenever something material changes — purpose, model, provider, the population of users being served, the data involved, geography, integration surface, the value of the transactions involved, scale, or the level of autonomy the system is permitted. Any one of those changes can shift the required assurance level even if nothing else about the system has changed, and a tailoring record that is never revisited quietly becomes a description of how the system used to be governed rather than how it actually needs to be governed now.
