# Chapter 04: Multiple Entry Paths and Configurable Depth

# Multiple Entry Paths and Configurable Depth

> **CHAPTER PURPOSE** Allow organizations to begin top-down, bottom-up, technology-led, compliance-led or from an existing solution, while converging on a common lifecycle, and give teams a disciplined way to compress the method without quietly removing the decisions it protects.

## Background and context

Chapter 3 described how enterprise direction is meant to be set, but in practice very few AI initiatives actually begin with a strategy document. They begin with a business problem someone is tired of solving manually, a proof of concept a vendor left behind, a platform investment already committed, a regulator's letter, or a production system that is quietly failing and needs attention now. A methodology that only recognizes the strategy-first case forces every one of those legitimate starting points through an artificial front door, which either delays initiatives that are already in motion or—more commonly—gets quietly bypassed, leaving the initiative running without the discipline the methodology was supposed to provide. This chapter exists to close that gap: it names six realistic entry paths, gives each one a first action appropriate to where it is actually starting from, and defines the point at which all six paths converge onto the same lifecycle and the same evidence expectations.

The other problem this chapter solves is the opposite failure mode: an organization that takes "multiple entry paths" as license to skip foundational work entirely, or that conflates simplifying an artifact with removing the decision it exists to protect. The second half of this chapter—entry normalization and controllable depth—is the answer to that risk, and it connects directly forward to Chapter 5, which assumes an initiative has already been correctly classified and normalized by the time it reaches portfolio assessment.

## Entry paths

OASIS does not require a strategy-first sequence, and treating strategy-first as mandatory would in practice exclude most of the initiatives an organization actually needs to govern. An organization may legitimately enter through a business problem, a local proof of concept, a platform investment, a compliance concern, a failing production solution, or an enterprise transformation mandate, and each of those starting points carries different available evidence and a different immediate risk. The method's first move, regardless of entry point, is diagnostic rather than prescriptive: identify the current state honestly, then fill in only the missing foundations needed to make the next decision safely—not the entire methodology from scratch, and not a superficial retrofit that leaves the real gaps in place.

Consider how differently that plays out across the six paths. A top-down strategic initiative typically already has executive sponsorship and a value pool in mind, but usually has not yet translated ambition into an owned outcome hypothesis someone can be held accountable for—so that translation is the first action, and the path converges once a prioritized portfolio position and a deployment sponsor exist. Bottom-up business demand is close to the opposite: the outcome and the process are real and immediate, but they are rarely baselined rigorously, so the first action is establishing that baseline and mapping the actual process, converging once an Outcome Charter, a risk track and a check for reusable capability are in place. A technology-led proof of concept is often the trickiest case in practice, because the demonstration has already created momentum and sometimes a sunk-cost attachment; the discipline required is to reverse-frame the demonstration into a measurable outcome and a representative evaluation before that momentum is allowed to carry it further, converging on an evidence-based decision to pilot or to stop. A regulatory or risk-driven initiative starts from an obligation rather than an opportunity, so the first action is mapping that obligation to concrete controls, evidence and accountable processes, converging on a governed workflow with an assurance plan. An existing production solution—often the least glamorous entry point and the one most likely to be neglected—requires an honest assessment of its outcomes, failures, controls, economics and service ownership before any further investment, converging on a remediation, renewal, scale or retirement plan. And a platform-led initiative, which proposes a shared capability before any single business demand has proven it out, needs that demand validated against multiple real deployments before it converges on a product roadmap backed by genuinely reusable use cases rather than a hypothetical one.

|-------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------|
| Top-down strategy            | Translate ambition and value pools into owned outcome hypotheses.                        | Prioritized portfolio and deployment sponsors.             |
| Bottom-up business demand    | Baseline the local outcome and map the real process.                                     | Outcome Charter, risk track and reusable capability check. |
| Technology-led PoC           | Reverse-frame the demonstration into a measurable outcome and representative evaluation. | Evidence-based pilot or stop decision.                     |
| Regulatory or risk need      | Map obligations to controls, evidence and accountable processes.                         | Governed workflow and assurance plan.                      |
| Existing production solution | Assess outcomes, failures, controls, economics and service ownership.                    | Remediation, renewal, scale or retirement plan.             |
| Platform-led initiative      | Validate shared capability demand against multiple real deployments.                     | Product roadmap backed by reusable use cases.               |

## Entry normalization

Whichever door an initiative walks through, the same five-step normalization sequence brings it onto common ground with everything else in the portfolio. It begins with honestly identifying the business outcome, the stakeholders it affects, and the current baseline—an identification that has to happen even when, and especially when, the initiative began life as a technology experiment with no outcome framing at all. From there, the initiative is classified by impact, autonomy, data sensitivity, exposure and scale, which is the classification that determines which assurance track it follows for the rest of its life. The initiative is then located within the six-phase lifecycle, and whatever prior evidence already exists is assessed for what can genuinely be reused rather than redone for the sake of process completeness. Only the minimum missing artifacts are created, each with an accountable owner assigned—not a full documentation set regenerated from a template. And the sequence closes by choosing the next real decision gate the initiative needs to pass, rather than resetting it to the beginning of the methodology as if none of its prior history counted.

1. Identify the business outcome, affected stakeholders and current baseline—even if the initiative began as a technology experiment.
2. Classify impact, autonomy, data sensitivity, exposure and scale to select the assurance track.
3. Locate the initiative in the six-phase lifecycle and assess which prior evidence is reusable.
4. Create the minimum missing artifacts and assign accountable owners.
5. Choose the next decision gate rather than restarting the entire methodology.

## Controllable depth

Depth is controlled through four specific levers, and naming them precisely matters because "make it lighter" is not itself a safe instruction—it has to be made concrete enough that a team knows what it is and is not allowed to compress. The four levers are role consolidation, artifact compression, gate combination and workstream activation. A small team applying all four might legitimately hold Engage & Align and Discover & Validate in a single workshop, capture the output in one combined Outcome-and-Risk Brief instead of separate documents, and let one person carry product, process and engineering responsibility rather than staffing three distinct roles. That is proportionate simplification, and it is exactly what Chapter 2's proportionate-assurance principle intends.

What the same team may not do, regardless of how small or fast-moving it is, is remove the outcome owner, the evidence threshold, the authority boundary, or the operational fallback. Those four elements are not artifacts to be trimmed for convenience; they are the decisions the artifacts exist to support, and an organization that compresses an Outcome Charter down to a paragraph has simplified packaging, while an organization that skips defining who owns the outcome has removed the decision itself. The distinction is the single most important discipline in this chapter, which is why it is worth stating as a standing rule rather than leaving it implicit.

> **RULE** Simplify the packaging; do not remove the decision that the artifact is meant to support.
