# Chapter 04: Multiple Entry Paths and Configurable Depth

# Multiple Entry Paths and Configurable Depth

> **CHAPTER PURPOSE** Allow organizations to begin top-down, bottom-up, technology-led, compliance-led or from an existing solution, while converging on a common lifecycle, and give teams a disciplined way to compress the method without quietly removing the decisions it protects.

## Background and context

Chapter 3 described how enterprise direction is meant to be set, but few AI initiatives actually begin with a strategy document. They begin with a business problem someone is tired of solving manually, a proof of concept a vendor left behind, a platform investment already committed, a regulator's letter, or a production system quietly failing. A methodology that only recognizes the strategy-first case forces every other starting point through an artificial front door—which delays initiatives already in motion or, more commonly, gets bypassed, leaving the initiative running without discipline. This chapter names six realistic entry paths, gives each a first action, and defines where all six converge onto the same lifecycle and evidence expectations.

The chapter also addresses the opposite failure mode: an organization treating "multiple entry paths" as license to skip foundational work, or conflating simplifying an artifact with removing the decision it protects. The second half—entry normalization and controllable depth—answers that risk, and connects forward to Chapter 5, which assumes an initiative has already been classified and normalized by the time it reaches portfolio assessment.

## Entry paths

OASIS does not require a strategy-first sequence; treating it as mandatory would exclude most initiatives an organization actually needs to govern. An organization may legitimately enter through a business problem, a local proof of concept, a platform investment, a compliance concern, a failing production solution, or an enterprise transformation mandate. Each carries different available evidence and immediate risk. The method's first move, regardless of entry point, is diagnostic: identify the current state honestly, then fill in only the missing foundations needed for the next decision—not the entire methodology from scratch, and not a superficial retrofit that leaves real gaps in place.

That plays out differently across the six paths. A top-down strategic initiative typically has executive sponsorship and a value pool in mind, but has not translated ambition into an owned outcome hypothesis—so that translation is the first action, converging once a prioritized portfolio position and deployment sponsor exist. Bottom-up business demand is close to the opposite: the outcome and process are real and immediate but rarely baselined rigorously, so the first action is establishing that baseline, converging once an Outcome Charter, risk track and reusable-capability check are in place. A technology-led proof of concept is often trickiest, because the demonstration has already created momentum and sometimes sunk-cost attachment; the discipline is to reverse-frame it into a measurable outcome and representative evaluation before that momentum carries it further, converging on a pilot-or-stop decision. A regulatory or risk-driven initiative starts from an obligation, so the first action is mapping that obligation to concrete controls and accountable processes, converging on a governed workflow with an assurance plan. An existing production solution—often neglected—requires an honest assessment of its outcomes, failures, controls, economics and ownership before further investment, converging on a remediation, renewal, scale or retirement plan. A platform-led initiative, proposing shared capability before demand is proven, needs that demand validated against multiple real deployments before converging on a product roadmap backed by genuinely reusable use cases.

| **Entry path**               | **First OASIS action**                                                                   | **Convergence point**                                      |
|-------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------|
| Top-down strategy            | Translate ambition and value pools into owned outcome hypotheses.                        | Prioritized portfolio and deployment sponsors.             |
| Bottom-up business demand    | Baseline the local outcome and map the real process.                                     | Outcome Charter, risk track and reusable capability check. |
| Technology-led PoC           | Reverse-frame the demonstration into a measurable outcome and representative evaluation. | Evidence-based pilot or stop decision.                     |
| Regulatory or risk need      | Map obligations to controls, evidence and accountable processes.                         | Governed workflow and assurance plan.                      |
| Existing production solution | Assess outcomes, failures, controls, economics and service ownership.                    | Remediation, renewal, scale or retirement plan.             |
| Platform-led initiative      | Validate shared capability demand against multiple real deployments.                     | Product roadmap backed by reusable use cases.               |

## Entry normalization

Whichever door an initiative walks through, the same five-step normalization sequence brings it onto common ground with the rest of the portfolio. It begins with honestly identifying the business outcome, affected stakeholders and current baseline—necessary even when the initiative began as a technology experiment with no outcome framing. The initiative is then classified by impact, autonomy, data sensitivity, exposure and scale, which sets its assurance track. It is located within the six-phase lifecycle, and prior evidence is assessed for what can genuinely be reused rather than redone for completeness. Only the minimum missing artifacts are created, each with an accountable owner. The sequence closes by choosing the next real decision gate, rather than resetting to the beginning as if prior history did not count.

1. Identify the business outcome, affected stakeholders and current baseline—even if the initiative began as a technology experiment.
2. Classify impact, autonomy, data sensitivity, exposure and scale to select the assurance track.
3. Locate the initiative in the six-phase lifecycle and assess which prior evidence is reusable.
4. Create the minimum missing artifacts and assign accountable owners.
5. Choose the next decision gate rather than restarting the entire methodology.

## Controllable depth

Depth is controlled through four specific levers: role consolidation, artifact compression, gate combination and workstream activation. Naming them precisely matters, because "make it lighter" is not a safe instruction on its own—it has to be concrete enough that a team knows what it can and cannot compress. A small team applying all four might hold Engage & Align and Discover & Validate in a single workshop, capture the output in one combined Outcome-and-Risk Brief, and let one person carry product, process and engineering responsibility rather than staffing three roles. That is proportionate simplification, exactly what Chapter 2's proportionate-assurance principle intends.

What the same team may not do, regardless of size or speed, is remove the outcome owner, the evidence threshold, the authority boundary, or the operational fallback. Those four elements are not artifacts to trim for convenience; they are the decisions the artifacts exist to support. An organization that compresses an Outcome Charter to a paragraph has simplified packaging. One that skips defining who owns the outcome has removed the decision itself—the single most important distinction in this chapter.

> **RULE** Simplify the packaging; do not remove the decision that the artifact is meant to support.
