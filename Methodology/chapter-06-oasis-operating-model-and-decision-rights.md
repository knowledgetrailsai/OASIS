# Chapter 06: OASIS Operating Model and Decision Rights

# OASIS Operating Model and Decision Rights

> **CHAPTER PURPOSE** Define how enterprise leadership, business domains, platform teams, governance functions, forward-deployed pods and service operations share accountability, and close the gap that decision rights left ambiguous elsewhere in the method most often cause in practice.

## Background and context

Chapters 3 through 5 described what an enterprise should fund, how initiatives enter the method, and how a portfolio should be assessed and balanced. None of that answers a question that determines whether any of it actually works: when a decision needs to be made—commit funding, release to production, expand autonomy, retire a service—who, specifically, has the authority to make it, and what evidence do they need in hand before they are entitled to make it? Ambiguous decision rights are one of the most common reasons AI initiatives stall or drift even after they clear every other gate described in this handbook. A model owner ends up making a business call by default, because nobody else was positioned to make it in the moment; or a business sponsor approves a production release without the technical evidence to know whether it is safe, because nobody defined what evidence that approval required. This chapter closes Part I of the handbook by making both the operating layers and the decision rights within them explicit, so that Part II's phase-by-phase lifecycle has a clear answer, at every gate, to "accountable to whom, on what evidence."

The layered operating model below and the decision-rights table that follows it are this chapter's two central contributions, and they are best read together: the layers describe who exists in the organization and what they are broadly accountable for, while the decision-rights table pins specific, consequential decisions to specific roles and specific evidence requirements. For the technical counterpart to this operating model—how agents themselves are typed, how they are permitted to collaborate, and where an agent's authority stops and a human's accountability begins—see [Architecture Perspective 2: Agent Architecture](../architecture/perspective-02-agent-architecture.md), which extends the accountability logic in this chapter down to the level of individual agent types and their escalation paths. And where the risk and regulatory obligations referenced throughout this chapter's decision-rights table need to be traced to specific external frameworks, the [Regulatory and Standards Framework Alignment Index](../references/regulatory-framework-alignment-index.md) is the reference that indexes those obligations by jurisdiction and standard.

## Operating layers

OASIS distributes accountability across seven layers, and the distribution is deliberate rather than incidental—each layer exists because the alternative, collapsing two of them together, has a predictable failure mode. Enterprise leadership carries the AI ambition, the investment envelope, enterprise outcomes and risk appetite; this is the layer Chapter 3 addressed directly. An AI Transformation Office or Center of Excellence sits below it, holding the portfolio, standards, capability building, governance coordination and value realization—the connective layer that keeps enterprise ambition from being reinterpreted differently by every business unit that touches it. The business domain owns the problem itself: the process decisions, the users, adoption, and ultimately the business outcome the whole initiative exists to move, which is why Chapter 5's opportunity framing insists on a named business owner rather than treating outcome ownership as implicit.

The enterprise intelligence platform layer owns the reusable model, context, knowledge, tool, evaluation, security and runtime services that individual deployments draw on rather than rebuild—the layer Chapter 3's platform row and Chapter 2's reuse-what-repeats principle both point toward. A forward-deployed pod carries operational discovery, vertical delivery, integration, activation and the transfer of what is learned back into the platform and the portfolio; this is typically the layer doing the day-to-day work of turning an outcome hypothesis into a working system. Service operations owns reliability, support, incidents, changes, assurance and the ongoing service economics once something is live—the layer that makes the "managed outcome service" from Chapter 1 a durable reality rather than a launch-day claim. And an independent risk or assurance function provides challenge, review and evidence validation wherever the risk profile of a decision warrants a genuinely separate set of eyes, distinct from the team that built the system being reviewed.

| **Layer**                        | **Primary accountability**                                                                |
|-------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Enterprise leadership            | AI ambition, investment envelope, enterprise outcomes and risk appetite.                  |
| AI Transformation Office / CoE   | Portfolio, standards, capability building, governance coordination and value realization. |
| Business domain                  | Problem ownership, process decisions, users, adoption and business outcomes.              |
| Enterprise intelligence platform | Reusable model, context, knowledge, tool, evaluation, security and runtime services.      |
| Forward-deployed pod             | Operational discovery, vertical delivery, integration, activation and learning transfer.  |
| Service operations               | Reliability, support, incidents, changes, assurance and service economics.                |
| Independent risk / assurance     | Challenge, review and evidence validation where risk warrants separation.                 |

## Decision rights

Naming the layers is only half the job; OASIS also separates four kinds of authority that are easy to blur together in practice—outcome ownership, technical service ownership, model or platform stewardship, and risk acceptance—and insists they be assigned to named roles and case classes rather than inferred from whoever happens to have technical control of the system. That last point is worth stating plainly, because it is the specific failure this section is designed to prevent: no model owner may become the de facto owner of a consequential business decision simply because they are the person closest to the system making it. Capability is not authority, and a methodology that lets the two collapse into each other has quietly handed governance to whoever built the tool, rather than to whoever is accountable for its consequences.

The table below pins six recurring, consequential decisions to an accountable role and the evidence that role should require before acting. Committing to an outcome sits with the Business Outcome Owner, on the strength of a baseline, a metric tree, a value logic, scope and guardrails—the same elements Chapter 1's outcome-contract construct and Chapter 5's hypothesis form both require, now converging on a single accountable signature. Proceeding to production engineering is a joint call between the Outcome Owner and the Product or Service Owner, made on the evidence of a validated vertical slice, a failure taxonomy, and an honest accounting of risk and economics—not on the strength of a demo. Releasing to live use sits with the Service Owner, with risk acceptance added where applicable, and requires production readiness, acceptance tests, a fallback path and a runbook, so that "it works in the demo" is never mistaken for "it is ready to operate."

Increasing autonomy is deliberately assigned to the business authority owner rather than to engineering, and requires live quality, override, incident, edge-case and control evidence—autonomy is expanded on the strength of what the system has actually done in production, not on confidence in what it is designed to do. Scaling or productizing a capability sits with the Portfolio or Platform Owner, evidenced by repeatability, economics, demonstrated demand and a clear isolation of which elements are genuinely configurable versus fixed. And retirement sits jointly with the Outcome and Service Owners, triggered by declining value, unacceptable risk, an available replacement, or a scheduled lifecycle decision—retirement being, as Chapter 2 established, a legitimate and sometimes necessary outcome rather than a failure to be avoided.

| **Decision**                      | **Accountable role**                             | **Evidence expected**                                                    |
|--------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Commit to outcome                 | Business Outcome Owner                           | Baseline, metric tree, value logic, scope and guardrails.                |
| Proceed to production engineering | Outcome Owner with Product/Service Owner         | Validated vertical slice, failure taxonomy, risk and economics.          |
| Release to live use               | Service Owner with Risk acceptance as applicable | Production readiness, acceptance tests, fallback and runbook.            |
| Increase autonomy                 | Business authority owner                         | Live quality, override, incident, edge-case and control evidence.        |
| Scale or productize               | Portfolio / Platform Owner                       | Repeatability, economics, demand and isolation of configurable elements. |
| Retire                            | Outcome and Service Owners                       | Declining value, unacceptable risk, replacement or lifecycle decision.   |

## Funding model

Decision rights and funding are two sides of the same accountability structure, and OASIS deliberately lets funding evolve across the horizons rather than committing a single budget line for the life of an initiative. Discovery funding is scoped narrowly to test genuine uncertainty—it should be small enough that stopping is easy and consequence-free when the evidence does not support continuing. Production funding creates the actual service once that uncertainty has been resolved in the initiative's favor. Run funding sustains outcomes on an ongoing basis, covering the operational reality that a managed outcome service, as described in Chapter 1, does not end at launch. And platform funding productizes capability that has proven itself repeatedly across multiple deployments, converting what began as project-specific spend into a shared enterprise asset.

The discipline that ties all four stages together is that continuation is tied to evidence and learning, not to sunk-cost protection—an initiative does not earn its next funding tranche because of what has already been spent on it, but because of what the evidence says it is worth continuing to spend on. Shared services deserve particular attention here: they should carry transparent allocation logic, so that a local deployment deciding whether to consume a shared platform capability or build its own bespoke alternative can actually compare the two honestly, rather than treating the shared service as a free good or an opaque tax. With decision rights and funding both made explicit, Part II of this handbook can turn from establishing how OASIS is organized to walking through how it is actually run, phase by phase, beginning with Engage & Align.
