# Common Challenges and Remedies

This reference maps recurring challenges faced by AI transformation teams to
the organizational change required and the mature remedy. The framework named
in each remedy identifies the OASIS ecosystem repository with the most relevant
guidance; use the matrix to route a challenge into deeper lifecycle, delivery,
engineering, governance, evaluation, operations, or economics material.

| Category / Area                       | Q — Challenge AI transformation teams face                                                                       | Change required                                                 | A — Mature remedy                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                              |
| ------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Transformation & Scaling**          | **1. We have many AI pilots, but how do we move them into enterprise adoption?**                                 | Shift from POC-centric delivery to a transformation lifecycle.  | Establish a stage-gated path from outcome definition through validation, productionisation, adoption, operations and scaling. Require evidence on value, technical readiness, risk, operating ownership and adoption before progressing. **(**OASIS** — provides the overarching transformation lifecycle and decision-gate structure.)**      |
| **Transformation & Scaling**          | **2. Different teams are starting AI from different places. Must everyone follow one rigid process?**            | Move from rigid methodology to governed flexibility.            | Create a common transformation backbone with different entry points for business, engineering, data, governance or operations, while ensuring every initiative ultimately meets common standards for value, risk, adoption and operational readiness. **(**OASIS** — provides the common lifecycle while allowing flexible entry and depth.)** |
| **Transformation & Scaling**          | **3. Nobody is clear who owns business outcomes, technology, risk and adoption.**                                | Establish explicit accountability and decision rights.          | Assign accountable owners for business outcome, product, technology, risk, adoption and operations. Define who can approve, escalate, stop or accept residual risk so accountability remains intact across functions. **(**OASIS** — establishes roles, governance checkpoints and outcome ownership.)**                                       |
| **Transformation & Scaling**          | **4. We launch AI but cannot tell whether transformation actually happened.**                                    | Move from activity metrics to outcome measurement.              | Establish baselines before implementation and measure business outcome, process performance, adoption, reliability, risk and economics after deployment. Scale only where there is demonstrable improvement. **(**OASIS** — connects transformation activities to measurable outcomes and scale decisions.)**                                  |
| **Opportunity & Portfolio**           | **5. We have hundreds of AI ideas. Which ones should we pursue?**                                                | Move from ideation to portfolio discipline.                     | Assess opportunities against strategic value, feasibility, data readiness, process suitability, risk, adoption effort and reuse potential. Prioritize those capable of materially changing economics, cycle time, quality or customer experience. **(**NEXUS** — structures, compares and prioritizes AI opportunities.)**                     |
| **Opportunity & Portfolio**           | **6. Business teams ask for “an AI assistant” without defining the real problem.**                               | Reframe technology requests as business problems.               | Decompose the request into workflow, decisions, information needs, bottlenecks, actors and desired outcomes. Only then determine whether the intervention should be an assistant, automation, agent, retrieval system, predictive model—or no AI. **(**NEXUS** — converts broad AI ideas into defined use cases and business problems.)**      |
| **Opportunity & Portfolio**           | **7. Teams either automate too little or immediately propose autonomous agents.**                                | Treat autonomy as a design variable.                            | Determine autonomy at task level. Begin with the minimum autonomy necessary to create value and increase it only when reliability, controls, reversibility and operational evidence justify doing so. **(**NEXUS** — determines automation potential; **LOOM** — defines how human and AI authority should evolve.)**                          |
| **Opportunity & Portfolio**           | **8. Attractive AI ideas enter the portfolio without measurable impact.**                                        | Link use cases to strategic outcomes.                           | Require every initiative to define affected capability, KPI, baseline, target, population and value hypothesis. Initiatives without a credible pathway to measurable outcomes should remain experiments rather than transformation investments. **(**NEXUS** — connects AI opportunities to capabilities, outcomes and KPIs.)**                |
| **Agentic Engineering**               | **9. Developers use AI coding tools differently and engineering becomes inconsistent.**                          | Institutionalise agentic engineering practices.                 | Define common engineering patterns for AI-assisted requirements, design, coding, review, testing and deployment. Standardize controls and evidence requirements while allowing flexibility within approved tooling. **(**AEGIS** — establishes enterprise patterns for AI-assisted and agentic software engineering.)**                        |
| **Agentic Engineering**               | **10. AI generates code rapidly, but review and quality become bottlenecks.**                                    | Redesign assurance for AI-accelerated development.              | Shift assurance left with automated testing, static analysis, dependency controls, policy checks and risk-based reviews. Focus human review on architecture, critical logic and high-impact changes. **(**AEGIS** — redesigns SDLC controls around AI-generated software.)**                                                                   |
| **Agentic Engineering**               | **11. Teams do not know how much freedom coding agents should receive.**                                         | Introduce risk-based engineering autonomy.                      | Define permitted actions by environment and risk—code generation, testing, branch creation, infrastructure changes or deployment—and strengthen sandboxing, approvals and oversight as potential impact increases. **(**AEGIS** — provides engineering governance patterns for agent permissions and autonomy.)**                              |
| **Agentic Engineering**               | **12. AI coding appears faster, but nobody knows whether productivity improved.**                                | Measure system-level engineering performance.                   | Evaluate cycle time, throughput, defect escape rate, rework, maintainability, review effort and developer experience together. Avoid treating generated-code volume as productivity. **(**AEGIS** — measures whether AI improves software-delivery outcomes.)**                                                                                |
| **Knowledge & Grounding**             | **13. The model performs poorly even though the underlying LLM is strong.**                                      | Treat knowledge quality as a first-class system component.      | Diagnose source authority, ingestion, metadata, retrieval, ranking, context assembly and freshness before replacing the model. Many apparent LLM failures are knowledge-system failures. **(**FORGE** — engineers and diagnoses the enterprise knowledge and grounding layer.)**                                                               |
| **Knowledge & Grounding**             | **14. Different AI applications create separate copies of organizational knowledge.**                            | Move toward reusable enterprise knowledge services.             | Organize knowledge into governed domains with authoritative sources, owners, permissions, metadata and lifecycle rules. Applications should consume shared knowledge services rather than repeatedly duplicating the same information. **(**FORGE** — structures reusable and governed knowledge domains.)**                                   |
| **Knowledge & Grounding**             | **15. Users cannot tell whether AI answers came from approved information.**                                     | Make provenance intrinsic to the experience.                    | Preserve traceability from answer to retrieved context and authoritative sources. Differentiate where useful between sourced facts, inferred content and generated content. **(**FORGE** — provides grounding, provenance and authoritative-source practices.)**                                                                               |
| **Knowledge & Grounding**             | **16. RAG works in demos but retrieval deteriorates in production.**                                             | Operate retrieval as a measurable subsystem.                    | Maintain retrieval test sets, measure relevance and coverage, detect stale knowledge and analyse retrieval failures separately from generation failures. Add retrieval complexity only when evidence supports it. **(**FORGE** — improves retrieval and context engineering; **VERITY** — provides systematic evaluation.)**                   |
| **Human–AI Workflow & Adoption**      | **17. Employees receive an AI chatbot, but the underlying process barely changes.**                              | Redesign work, not merely the interface.                        | Reconstruct end-to-end workflows around what AI should perform, what humans should retain and what systems can automate. Remove redundant steps rather than adding AI as another task in the existing process. **(**LOOM** — redesigns human-AI workflows, roles and handoffs.)**                                                              |
| **Human–AI Workflow & Adoption**      | **18. Employees do not know when they should trust AI.**                                                         | Build calibrated reliance rather than unconditional trust.      | Communicate uncertainty, evidence and limitations. Define where AI can be relied upon, where verification is required, and when users should escalate or override. **(**LOOM** — shapes human reliance and interaction; **VERITY** — supplies evidence supporting the appropriate level of trust.)**                                           |
| **Human–AI Workflow & Adoption**      | **19. Management wants autonomy immediately, while users are uncomfortable.**                                    | Make autonomy progressive and evidence-based.                   | Progress from assist → recommend → act with approval → exception-based execution → autonomous execution only when reliability, control maturity and user readiness support progression. **(**LOOM** — designs progressive autonomy and human authority.)**                                                                                     |
| **Human–AI Workflow & Adoption**      | **20. Training happens only at go-live and adoption remains weak.**                                              | Treat adoption as organizational redesign.                      | Begin role redesign, capability building, communication, incentives and manager engagement while the solution is being designed. Train people on the new way of working, not merely the AI interface. **(**LOOM** — supports adoption, role redesign, capability building and behavioural change.)**                                           |
| **Evaluation & Reliability**          | **21. The demo looks excellent. How do we know the system is genuinely production-ready?**                       | Move from demonstration quality to evidence-based assurance.    | Define acceptance criteria before implementation and evaluate representative tasks, edge cases, workflow completion, failure recovery and business outcomes before release. **(**VERITY** — establishes evaluation evidence and production-readiness thresholds.)**                                                                            |
| **Evaluation & Reliability**          | **22. Teams report one “accuracy” figure that hides important failures.**                                        | Evaluate quality as a multidimensional construct.               | Measure correctness, groundedness, completeness, consistency, safety, latency, instruction adherence and task success, weighted according to business criticality. **(**VERITY** — defines multidimensional AI quality and reliability measurement.)**                                                                                         |
| **Evaluation & Reliability**          | **23. Evaluation datasets perform well but do not represent production reality.**                                | Make evaluation data representative and continuously refreshed. | Build datasets from real workflow distributions, difficult cases, edge conditions and known failures. Maintain protected benchmarks while continually incorporating meaningful production cases. **(**VERITY** — engineers representative evaluation datasets and benchmarks.)**                                                               |
| **Evaluation & Reliability**          | **24. How do we prevent regressions as prompts, models and knowledge change?**                                   | Treat evaluation as part of release management.                 | Version prompts, models, knowledge configuration, tools and policies together; run regression suites on material changes and block releases when critical dimensions deteriorate. **(**VERITY** — detects behavioural regressions; **HELM** — embeds those checks into release management.)**                                                  |
| **Governance, Risk & Responsible AI** | **25. Governance reviews happen at the end and delay deployment.**                                               | Move governance into design and engineering.                    | Convert security, privacy, Responsible AI and regulatory expectations into lifecycle requirements addressed during architecture and engineering rather than through late-stage approvals. **(**COMPASS** — translates governance principles and risks into implementable controls.)**                                                          |
| **Governance, Risk & Responsible AI** | **26. Every AI use case receives the same governance treatment.**                                                | Adopt proportionate, risk-based governance.                     | Classify use cases according to impact, autonomy, sensitivity, reversibility and affected stakeholders, and increase controls, evaluation and human oversight as risk rises. **(**COMPASS** — provides risk classification and proportionate control design.)**                                                                                |
| **Governance, Risk & Responsible AI** | **27. Agentic AI can take actions; traditional Responsible AI controls are insufficient.**                       | Govern actions and autonomy, not only outputs.                  | Control agent identity, permissions, tool access, transaction boundaries and escalation. Introduce approvals, logging, kill switches and recovery mechanisms for high-impact actions. **(**COMPASS** — extends governance to agent autonomy, permissions, actions and accountability.)**                                                       |
| **Governance, Risk & Responsible AI** | **28. Compliance becomes documentation disconnected from engineering.**                                          | Operationalise policy as controls and evidence.                 | Convert policies and regulations into specific technical and procedural requirements that can be implemented, tested, monitored and evidenced. Maintain traceability from obligation through control to assurance result. **(**COMPASS** — bridges regulation, governance, engineering controls and assurance evidence.)**                     |
| **AI Operations & Observability**     | **29. The AI goes live and nobody can explain what the agent actually did.**                                     | Make end-to-end observability mandatory.                        | Capture model/version, context, retrieved evidence, tools invoked, actions, failures, interventions, latency, cost and outcome so important decisions can be reconstructed. **(**HELM** — provides runtime observability and operational traceability.)**                                                                                      |
| **AI Operations & Observability**     | **30. A prompt, model or tool change unexpectedly reduces quality.**                                             | Treat AI behaviour as configuration-controlled software.        | Maintain versioned release manifests, regression testing, staged rollouts, canary populations and rollback mechanisms across all behaviour-shaping components. **(**HELM** — governs operational releases; **VERITY** — supplies regression evidence.)**                                                                                       |
| **AI Operations & Observability**     | **31. During incidents, teams cannot tell whether the problem is model, knowledge, tool, workflow or security.** | Develop an AI-specific operating model.                         | Establish failure taxonomy, ownership and response runbooks across model, data, retrieval, orchestration, tools, infrastructure, security and workflow layers. **(**HELM** — supports AI-specific incident detection, diagnosis, response and recovery.)**                                                                                     |
| **AI Operations & Observability**     | **32. Production feedback is collected but rarely improves the system.**                                         | Build a closed learning loop.                                   | Convert incidents, corrections, overrides and low-confidence cases into structured inputs for evaluation, knowledge improvement, workflow redesign and subsequent releases. **(**HELM** — captures operational signals; **VERITY** — converts them into evaluation and regression evidence.)**                                                 |
| **AI Economics & Value**              | **33. AI costs increase unexpectedly after adoption.**                                                           | Engineer economics into the solution from the beginning.        | Model complete workflow economics—including model usage, context, retrieval, tools, agent loops, infrastructure, evaluation and human intervention—and continuously compare actual economics with approval assumptions. **(**FULCRUM** — models and governs end-to-end AI economics.)**                                                        |
| **AI Economics & Value**              | **34. Management can measure AI spend but cannot establish value.**                                              | Establish outcome-based value accounting.                       | Link AI consumption to measurable operational or commercial outcomes. Maintain both cost and value ledgers so investment decisions consider net value rather than infrastructure expense alone. **(**FULCRUM** — connects AI cost with realized business value.)**                                                                             |
| **AI Economics & Value**              | **35. Teams optimize token price but ignore process economics.**                                                 | Optimize the whole workflow, not an individual model call.      | Measure cost per completed task or successful business outcome, including retries, validation, latency, failures and human effort. Optimize the economic system rather than only model price. **(**FULCRUM** — evaluates unit economics and value per successful outcome.)**                                                                   |
| **AI Economics & Value**              | **36. Nobody knows when an AI use case should scale, redesign or retire.**                                       | Introduce explicit portfolio economics.                         | Periodically assess value realization, adoption, reliability, risk and lifecycle cost. Scale where marginal value remains strong, redesign where bottlenecks are fixable and retire where economics no longer justify operation. **(**FULCRUM** — provides economic evidence; **OASIS** — incorporates it into scale/stop decisions.)**        |
| **Architecture & Model Strategy**     | **37. Teams automatically assume the largest frontier model is the best choice.**                                | Make architecture workload-driven.                              | Start with workload requirements—reasoning, multimodality, context, latency, deployment, security and economics—and select the simplest architecture capable of meeting them reliably. **(**AXIOM** — guides model and architecture selection based on workload characteristics.)**                                                            |
| **Architecture & Model Strategy**     | **38. Business and technical teams use model, RAG, agent and architecture interchangeably.**                     | Establish a common system architecture language.                | Separate model, knowledge, orchestration, tool, workflow, integration and human-interaction layers so architectural decisions can be discussed independently and clearly. **(**AXIOM** — establishes architectural concepts, boundaries and common terminology.)**                                                                             |
| **Architecture & Model Strategy**     | **39. Teams struggle to compare alternative AI architectures.**                                                  | Evaluate architectures against enterprise quality attributes.   | Compare options across quality, reasoning capability, latency, resilience, security, portability, observability, operational complexity and total cost, documenting significant trade-offs explicitly. **(**AXIOM** — provides the architecture decision and trade-off lens.)**                                                                |
| **Architecture & Model Strategy**     | **40. Architecture choices quickly become obsolete as new models emerge.**                                       | Architect for substitutability and evolution.                   | Decouple model access, knowledge, orchestration, tools and business workflows through stable interfaces. Minimize provider-specific dependencies so technologies can evolve independently. **(**AXIOM** — supports modular, evolvable AI architecture and emerging architecture choices.)**                                                    |
